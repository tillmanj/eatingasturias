{% newthought 'Europeans, specifically the Spanish,' %} first encountered corn approximately 7000 years after it was first cultivated. Quite possibly the single most important crop in the [Columbian Exchange](/culture/history/early-modern/columbian-exchange.html), maíz took a very long time to take hold in Spain, and once it did, that hold proved to be tenuous.

## First Encounters
The first recorded mention of corn by Europeans is subject to a minor dispute among historians. Quite a number of historians point to a journal entry made by Cristopher Columbus on October 16, 1492 on the *Isle of Hispañola* (today Haiti and the Dominican Republic).[^1] An equally large number prefer a slightly later date, contending that this journal entry is too vague. Rodrigo de Jerez and Luis Torres were sent on November 2, 1492 to look inland for a city on the island of Cuba. Three days later they returned with no location of a city, but with both tobacco and corn (gifts from natives they encountered), according to Columbus’ journal entry for November 6.[^2]

The first European study of corn that can be considered rigorous is that of Gonzalo Fernández de Oviedo y Valdés (hereafter simply "Fernández de Oviedo") in 1537.[^3] In it he devotes an entire chapter to a description of corn, including both its cultivation and use. It is noteworthy that he reports on a possible form of *nixtamalization* in this chapter without recognizing it or understanding it, stating simply that the women “grind it on a stone…adding water and letting it sit for some time…”[^4] If that water had been lime water (calcium hydroxide) produced from running water through ashes, this might be the first European description of the process of rendering corn into a dough that contains bioavailable niacin, instead of one that will lead to an amino acid deficiency, as we will see later.

As it stands, the first Spanish description of nixtamalization is the 1568 *Relación de las cosas de Yucatán* of Diego de Landa, a Franciscan friar known chiefly for destroying 99% of written Maya history. In this work he writes “…and the Indian women put the corn to soak in lime and water the night before, and that in the \[next\] morning it is soft and half cooked”[^5]

## Importation to Asturias
The story of how corn makes its way from the Americas to Asturias is, unfortunately, little more than exactly that; a story. There is a notable scarcity of works on corn’s introduction to northern Spain in general, and Asturias in particular. This is frustrating given the abundance of records that exist for both Portugal and France.

{% marginnote 'mn01' 'There is no shortage of local newspapers and "historians" who repeat this spurious claim to this day. [Here is a recent example](https://www.lavozdegalicia.es/noticia/amarina/2018/04/15/maiz-america-planto-primera-vez-europa-mondonedo-tapia/0003_201804X15C6993.htm) from La Voz de Galicia claiming this as the first planting of corn in all of Europe.'  %} The accepted Asturian legend around the introduction of corn is that Gonzalo Méndez de Cancio y Donlebún, the seventh governor of the Spanish province of La Florida from 1596–1603, brought three chest of seed corn home to Spain with him when he was replaced as governor of Florida in 1603 and planted the crop in his fields in the village of Bría in Tapia de Casareigo. 

Unfortunately for the legend, there is general agreement among historians that corn was planted as a staple crop (as opposed to an ornamental plant) in a wide swath from Coimbra through northern Portugal and into Galicia by the year 1525. [^6] By 1533, corn was being sold on local commodity exchanges in that area.

There is a single scholarly source on corn in Asturias and the wider north of Spain, but it is based primarily on the received wisdom above, and does not address the lack of evidence.[^7] This stands in opposition to work from the 1940s that detailed corn cultivation in the Basque country in 1576.[^8] Thus, we can dispense with the idea that Tapia de Casareigo is the cradle of corn cultivation in Europe, and more closely, in Spain.

{% marginfigure 'mf01' '/assets/images/history/corn domestication map.jpg' 'corn diffusion routes from N. America to Europe, from Tenaillon & Charcosset 2011[^9]' %}However, Méndez de Cancio existed, returned to Asturias, and indeed planted corn.[^10] And there is some good genetic evidence to support the introduction of maize into northern Spain as a separate event from the introduction into Portugal and southern Spain earlier.[^11]

Indeed, corn was so popular in northern Spain that by 1730, it had almost completely replaced local millet (*Setaria italica L.*) as the staple grain. Unfortunately the historical record is nearly silent on that period in Asturias, so we know almost nothing and can infer little more about the changes in agricultural practice, and culture more widely, as corn conquered the region.
## Sickness
Unfortunately, one of the things that made its way to Europe in the [Columbian Exchange](/culture/history/early-modern/columbian-exchange.html) was a new disease; one not seen in Europe before. Caused by a lack of vitamin B3 (niacin) and characterized by the “four d’s” (dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia, and death), pellagra came with corn, as a consequence of two converging drives.

On the one hand, corn is a massively productive crop, and it grows more readily than wheat in the types of climates found in northern Spain. This means that as soon as it was accepted for planting and eating, it became **the** staple crop in Asturias. ‘Simplicity of cultivation, low soil quality requirements, high yields and durability during the storage process made it popular among the native inhabitants of America, and after Columbus’ second expedition in 1494 – also Europeans...’[^12]

On the other hand, the niacin in corn is not bioavailable in its natural state. It is necessary to process the corn in specific ways to make the niacin more available. This process, now called *nixtamalization*{% sidenote 'sn01' 'The native American process was to rehydrate the dried corn by boiling it in a solution of wood ash. This creates lime water and makes the niacin bioavailable. the name for the process is comprised of two Nahuatl terms: _nixtli_ meaning “ashes”, and _tamalli_ meaning “cooked maíz masa.”[^13]' %}, was common in the Americas, but did not make the trip back across the Atlantic to Europe.

**Why** *nixtamalization* did not travel to Europe with the plant itself is tied up in a particular attitude towards food that was common in Spain in the 15<sup>th</sup> and 16<sup>th</sup> centuries. It was widely believed that the foods that they ate were the main determinant of their “european-ness” and their “spanish-ness”. If Spaniards ate Amerindian foods in Amerindian ways, they would turn into Amerindians, according to the theory. Conversely, to become “civilized” (European), the Amerindians needed to eat European foods.{% sidenote 'sn02' 'There is an absolutely fascinating book-length exploration of this food-based racial identity in the early modern Spanish mind by Rebecca Earle that is well worth checking out.[^14]' %} So, instead of being an intellectual superiority complex (*these savages don’t know anything about cooking so we can ignore their cooking techniques*) it was more of a fear of the Other (*if we cook like these people we will become them and no longer be Us*) that meant that *nixtamalization* was not pursued as a food processing technology in Spain.

The lack of this processing, coupled with the rapid spread of corn as a dietary staple among the poor of northern Spain in the 18<sup>th</sup> century gave rise to a (then) mystery disease. Sufferers developed a strange dermatitis, a red crust on any skin exposed to the sun. This was called by the locals *la mal de la rosa*.

Gaspar Casal, physician to the king Philip V, as sent to Oviedo in 1730. Not long after he began noticing the malady, and took an interest in it, assuming it to be a novel expression of leprosy.[^12] He also made tentative connections between the diet of the peasants (much corn, little meat) and the disease, but was unable to make a diagnosis. That definitive link would have to wait until 1810, when Dr. Giovanni Battista Marzari in Italy made the correlation clear. However, the correlation had been doing its work on the popularity of corn as a foodstuff even without objective facts to back it up. 

As pellagra spread across Spain and the rest of corn-consuming Europe, the grain fell out of fashion. The assumption was that corn contained some sort of toxin. The method of nixtamalization was as firmly forgotten as it had been rejected. In a few places, like Asturias, corn growing, and corn dishes, hung on as minor players in the diet. Now in Asturias corn is an ever-present staple, but not in the overwhelming quantities that expose people to pellagra.

## To The Present Day
For the most part, that situation continues to this day. Corn is grown in great quantities in Asturias, especially in the west, but almost none of it is destined for the human food chain. Animal feed and silage is the primary use, and the expressions of corn in Asturian cuisine are the exception, not the rule.

There is a lot less fresh corn eaten here than in the States, but compared to most of Spain, or even most of Europe, it is a corn lovers’ paradise. Fariñes, corn bread, corn fritters, even corn cookies and a corn cake can be found among the recipes of the region.

 Among them, the [torto](/recipes/breads/tortos-de-maíz.html) is the most widely eaten, followed by [Fariñes](/recipes/breakfast/fariñes.html) and the bread known as Boroña.
